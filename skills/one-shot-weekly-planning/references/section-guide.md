# The report: what each section is for

*Rev 23, 2026-09-21. Adapted from the weekly-report section guide v2, with the owner's architecture decisions and what the daily skill learned folded in. Voice: `writing-standard.md`.*

## The job

The person runs several timelines at once: their life, the year, the quarter, the month, the week, deadlines other people set, and commitments their own plans can't see. Different speeds, in conflict, unholdable in one head. Decode all of it into one legible picture of what matters and what doesn't.

## The two tests

- Postmortem: *"that describes last week perfectly."*
- Plan: *"you understand exactly what I need to do, and you showed me the forest instead of the trees."*

A section that serves neither is cut.

**Everything below is a default with its reason, not a rule.** You have read this person's material whole and you hold the writing standard. When the week in front of you calls for something else, do that, and note in the working document what you changed and why. The final call is yours.

**Recommended sizes, and why.** The opening promises about thirty minutes of their time, and reading is part of it. A report of 2,500 to 2,900 words of prose reads in about eleven minutes, and the people who tested this said plainly that a longer one was too much to take in. Tables sit on top of that and are scanned, not read; a table earns its place only when every row is an action the person approves or a number they would otherwise have to take on trust. As a guide for the prose, not a cap: section 1 about 475 words, section 2 about 375, section 3 about 425, section 4 about 525, section 5 about 525, section 6 about 375. The report ends by offering to go deeper on any section, so a section does not need to hold everything you found; it needs to hold what they must see to decide. A week with a real crisis in it earns more; a quiet week earns less. If a section runs to double its size, check whether you are explaining what a sentence could state, or arguing a finding that already has a home in another section.

## Applies to every section

- **Container is not the thing.** Tasks are a container; outcomes are the thing. Calendar events are a container; the story of the week is the thing. Hours are a container; what they can do with them is the thing. Translate before writing.
- **Open by orienting, then two clear moves.** A person briefing them, not a document declaring.
- **Carry the arc.** Each section says what its material does to the life they're building. Section 1 sets that arc; everything after is written with section 1 in hand.
- **Every conflict gets a call and a cost.** Competing pressures are never harmonious.
- **On their side.** You are their chief of staff, not their auditor. Say what is true without building a case against them. *Not:* "For two weeks your record has been dark." *But:* "Two things from last Tuesday are still open."
- **Name things the way they name them.** Use the words on their calendar and in their answers. *Not:* "Thursday was a delivery day with no room in it." *But:* "Thursday was client calls until two, then the school concert." If you would not say the phrase out loud to them, it does not go in.
- **Say the whole thing.** A sentence that points at something ("the ask," "that block," "it") without naming it makes them reconstruct your meaning. Name it, even if you named it a section ago.
- **Organize by the question the reader is asking, not by how you searched.** The way you found something is not the way to hand it over.
- **Separate items are a list. Reasoning is prose.** Five facts about who is home this week are five lines, not a paragraph. This is the rule the opening breaks most often: three timelines, two conflicts, four wins are separate items, and written as paragraphs they read as a wall.
- **The opening is where density costs the most.** A reader who is one minute in has no momentum yet, and sections 1 and 2 are where they decide whether this is worth half an hour. Keep the paragraphs there under about eighty words, put a bold lead on each one so the eye has somewhere to land, and never run three long paragraphs in a row. A paragraph past a hundred words in section 1 or 2 is a list that has not been broken out yet.
- **Never hand them a question the records can answer, and never carry a commitment they did not make.** Every commitment in the report traces to their words, their calendar, their tasks or their plans. If it came from your own inference, mark it or drop it.
- **A block is what it really is, not what it is called.** A slot marked free so others can book into it, a hold, a focus block with nothing in it: these are open time. Count capacity from what the person told you those blocks are, say which you treated as open, and if you had to guess, say that you guessed.
- **A calendar entry is a booking, not proof.** It tells you what was planned, and it is the best record you have, but only the person can tell you an event happened or how it went. Write "the surgery was on the calendar Friday 7 to 11 AM," not "she had surgery and came home at 11." Never add a detail (a time, an outcome, who did what) that no source gave you, and never give a clock time to something the person did not time. A recurring task checked off may only have been moved along; do not score it as done work. The same goes for absences: an event missing from the calendar does not tell you why it is missing. When the person declines to tell you something, that is an instruction to leave it unstated, not a gap to fill.
- **When two sources disagree, say so.** If their answer says the 10th and the calendar says the 11th, print both and say which one you used. If their answer names three people and their plan names two, say that too. Never pick silently; a silent pick is an error they can't catch.
- **Find what no single source says.** The report exists where two sources combine into a third fact.
- **Life first.** The week is a life with work in it, not a work week with a family attached. Guests, a surgery, a birthday, a performance: they belong in the picture even when they're not problems to solve.
- **Mark inferences.** Confident sentences are believable only when the uncertain ones are marked.
- **Sections 2 to 5 open with a story paragraph** that stands on its own: if the reader stopped there they would still know what the section found and what to do. It is the section's conclusion told as a story, written after the analysis, not a preview. Section 1 is already a story; section 6 is the story of the week.
- Read once Friday, re-entered midweek, archived as the month's record.

**1 — Where you are.** *Who am I, what game am I playing, what's my plan, what's pulling against what.*

- *The mirror, told as an arc:* who they are and what they're playing for, where they've been, what it keeps costing them, where they are now, where they're heading. Past, present, future. The test is recognition ("that's exactly me"), not flattery; the unflattering parts stay in.
- *The plan at every altitude:* long term, this year, this quarter, this month, this week, each in your own words for what that level is for. Digested, never copied from their documents. The week is the last rung and the bridge into the Win.
- *Timelines:* the ones they set, the ones others set, the ones they can't see. **A list of three, not three paragraphs.** The third group holds the most value; invisible commitments arrive as emergencies. Each with its position and its live unresolved question.
- *Conflicts:* name each one, make the call, show the cost. **A list, one per conflict.**
- Done when they could read only this and know what kind of week it is.

**2 — Last week.** *What happened, and what it did to my position.*

- **Tell it from the calendar first.** The calendar, titles and descriptions both, is usually the most reliable record of what a week was. Completed tasks come second; many people do real work without closing a task, and a burst of closures is often a cleanup, not a week's output. A "not sure" from the person never erases what the calendar shows, and the calendar never proves more than a booking (see above).
- Narrative, not a log. Name the kind of week, not each entry. Score against outcomes and timelines, never a task count.
- Wins, losses, tradeoffs, patterns. Headings say "last week."
- The lesson appears only if they stated one in their answers, quoted as theirs. If you have one to offer, offer it marked as yours. Never print a lesson "in their words" that they did not say.
- Score honestly, including the discount: real work that is smaller than it looks gets said.
- What it cost, and whether the cost was chosen. What carries forward and why it's still open.

**3 — The Win, and the aims behind it.** *The one thing, why it, what makes it survivable.*

- Chosen with leverage slightly ahead of urgency: the big things that must move, not the loud ones.
- One sentence, in the frame the person would use for their own week, then: specific, done-means, hours, relevance, deadline, checkpoint.
- Name which done-when actually proves it; most can be true while the thing is useless.
- State the strongest objection and answer it with a diagnosis, not optimism.
- Explaining the Win in paragraphs is the commonest way this section triples in size. By now they know why; what they need is what and when.
- Bonus aims get the same treatment, plus which one is sacrificed if the week breaks.
- Decisions made on their behalf: only the ones they might flip, one line of reasoning each, each flippable in a word. A calendar change is listed once, in section 4's changes table; this list holds only the decisions that are not calendar changes. What was set aside, and why.

**4 — Calendar clarity.** *What is my week without looking at it.*

- Lead with what's in the week, the life and the work. Capacity is one part, not the frame. Never walk it day by day.
- The life in it as a short list: who is home, who is travelling, what the evenings and the weekend hold.
- Which few events matter out of a hundred-plus, and why.
- Look for collisions (two things in one hour, duplicates, work booked against family), blind spots (events whose real consequence isn't on the calendar), combinations (two entries producing a constraint neither states), and what is coming just past the week, where the worst day often hides. **Those are lenses for finding problems, never headings.** Hand over what you find as one list, each item the problem and then what to do about it. *For example:* "**The 3 PM focus block on Tuesday isn't free.** Two calls already sit inside it. Delete it."
- Then blocks to protect by name, the changes table with reasons, and the capacity table. This is the section that runs long, and it runs long by saying things twice: a block that appears in the changes table does not also need a line in the protect list, a problem in the fix list does not get re-explained in the table's reason column, and the capacity table needs no paragraph restating it. Each thing once, in the place the reader will look for it. Real capacity: family, meals, school runs and routine are never work and never free, and when a work calendar and a family calendar stack, an hour is counted once.

**5 — Task analysis.** *What am I trying to accomplish, and what moves it.*

- Sort every task into outcomes before writing, and open by saying it: the list has this many tasks, and they are really this few outcomes. Name the outcomes; that is the picture. Expect to find that errands are one serious outcome, two projects are one, several tasks are one afternoon, and the biggest outcome has no time behind it.
- The Eisenhack matrix, as a table: leverage against urgency, every task that matters placed. This is the owner's own tool and it stays.
- Per outcome: what it is, deadline, position, its tasks, the real blocker, the cost if it stalls.
- The tasks that matter this week, however many that is; what's threatening to bury them; what's stale; what gets pushed, folded, handed off or closed. Close what serves nothing rather than rescheduling; repeated rescheduling is the signal.
- Protect the class nobody chases them about; it loses by default and the report is the only pressure.
- A question too large to be a task gets a working session with a date.
- State the sort. It is theirs to overrule, so every push and close is listed, but don't hand the sorting back as "suggestions."
- Never: due-date order.

**6 — Conclusion.** *Why this week matters, what I decide, what will go wrong, and how I walk in.*

- Written last and alone, after reading sections 1 to 5 back. It is not a sixth part; it is what the other five add up to. Collapse the report to one idea and let the themes hang off it; do not recap section by section. Reconnect the week to the year and the life.
- The week in one breath, then beat by beat where the beats carry meaning.
- Decisions owed, each with a deadline and the consequence of silence.
- What is most likely to get in the way this week, said plainly, why it will feel harmless at the time, and what to do when it does.
- Going into the week: where their time pays off, what to watch for, and encouragement a grounded coach would give. Never cheesy.
- Close on meaning. One ending. Then the one line offering to go deeper on any section. The choice comes after, in the chat.
- Never re-narrate. Never title it "What I need from you."

## Before returning

Every number computed by running code, every inference marked, every conflict carrying a call and a cost, every section tied to the arc, nothing they already knew, no detail no source gave, nothing with other people on it moved without asking. Times in the 12-hour clock. Postmortem passes test 1, plan passes test 2.
