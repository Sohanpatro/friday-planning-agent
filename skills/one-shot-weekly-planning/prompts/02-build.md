# Step 2: Build the week. No questions in this step.

**Purpose.** Turn everything you now hold, the harvest, their plans, and the five answers, into the report: six sections a person recognizes as their own life and their own week, at the quality of a chief of staff who understood it completely. The person is away getting coffee. You work alone until the report is done.

**Why it's built this way.** A writer cannot grade its own draft. So the whole report goes in front of two fresh readers who had no part in writing it, one for the writing and one for the facts, and you fix what they find. A wrong number in the first paragraph costs more trust than a clumsy sentence, and a writer is as blind to its own wrong number as to its own clumsy sentence.

## A. Think first. Write nothing for the reader yet.

Read `references/section-guide.md` and `references/writing-standard.md` whole. Then do the analysis into the working document, all of it, before a single section is written. The working document holds computed numbers, findings as short notes, and decisions. It never holds a draft of the report's prose; anything written as a paragraph here gets written a second time in a section, and that is wasted time:

- **The Win.** One, chosen with leverage slightly ahead of urgency, for the most gain across the most sides of their life and the arc they already chose. What was on the table, one honest attack per candidate, what survived, what's set aside. Two bonus aims, sources named, and which is sacrificed first.
- **Every decision you make for them,** one line each. They flip any of these in a word.
- **Last week, from the calendar.** Build last week from the calendar's titles and descriptions before anything else; it is usually the truest record of a week. Task completions and the person's answers add to it and correct it. They never replace it. And a calendar entry is a booking, not proof: it never licenses a detail no source gave.
- **The calendar read.** Real capacity, computed as a union of intervals: family, meals, school runs and routine are never work and never free, and when a work calendar and a family calendar stack, an hour is counted once. If a worker's index says there are no free hours all week, that is almost always this double count; recompute it yourself before you believe it. The few events that matter. Collisions, blind spots, combinations. The landmine just past the week. Blocks to protect, changes with reasons. Nothing with attendees moves.
- **The task sort.** Every open task into an outcome. The matrix, leverage against urgency. What matters this week, what's burying it, what's stale, what's pushed, folded, handed off, closed. The class nobody chases them about.
- **The life.** Who's in the house this week, who's sick, who's performing, what's a birthday. Not problems. The picture.
- **Numbers, dates and weekdays computed in code, into one file.** A wrong weekday ends the trust. Write one script, `facts.py`, that prints to `facts.md`: today and the plan week, each date with its weekday; the days and weeks from today to every deadline you intend to mention; the capacity table, with holds closed and with holds open; the task counts; every other figure you plan to use. Script output only, never typed. When you write, every number, date and weekday is copied from `facts.md`; if you want one that is not there, add it to the script and run it again. **That includes a number you would otherwise add up in your head** — two holds totalling thirteen hours, the outcomes' task counts totalling the open tasks, the hours in the Win totalling the hours in the changes table. A total typed by eye is the commonest wrong number in this report, it appears in three or four places once written, and it reads as computed. If a number is a sum of other numbers, `facts.py` does the sum. Never "correct" a figure from their documents without quoting the line you're correcting; if the two count different things, say so instead.
- **Where their answers and the records disagree, found by code.** Take every date, weekday, clock time and person's name in `answers.md` and search the index files for each one with code. Print the list: found where, or not found, or found with a different title or date. Every mismatch goes in the report with both sides, and every not-found is a claim only their word supports. This takes a minute and it is where the report's most valuable finding usually is.
- **Your own numbers are marked as yours, tables included.** An hour estimate, a checkpoint you invented, a length you guessed at: these belong in the plan, and each one says it is your estimate. A number in a table reads as a fact from the record; that is where an unmarked guess does the most damage.
- **What no source gave, you do not write.** Not that an event happened, not how it went, not a clock time for something the person did not time, not who was there unless the record names them. A checked-off recurring task may have been checked off to move it along. A doctor's or health appointment is written as booked, never as done or how it went. Something being in their Life Map does not make it this week's priority; their answers say what carries weight now.
- **Family, meals, workouts, kids, sleep and routine are never free time.** A free hour is one with nothing on it at all.

Go until you could state each section's conclusion in one plain sentence. Only then write. More context from the person makes the report sharper, not longer: use what they told you to choose better, and quote them where their words carry it, but the document doesn't grow because they talked more.

Nothing you learn here is written to memory. The report is the record.

## B. Write the whole report, then two fresh readers at once, then one fix pass

First, run `cat references/writing-standard.md references/layout.md how-i-write.md` so all three land in this conversation again. Reading them earlier in this session does not count; the words have to be in front of you. Nothing comes between that output and the first sentence of section 1.

Sections, in this order, from `references/section-guide.md`: 1 Where you are · 2 Last week · 3 The Win · 4 Calendar clarity · 5 Task analysis · 6 Conclusion. Section 1 first; everything after is written with section 1 in hand. Each finding is argued once, in the section it belongs to; any other section names it in a clause and moves on.

1. **Write all six sections**, one file each in the working folder, `sections/N.md`, in order, each written with the ones before it in hand. Save them in three groups: sections 1 to 3, then sections 4 and 5, then section 6 by itself. Before you write section 6, run `python3 scripts/check.py readback` and read what comes back. It prints sections 1 to 5 whole and records that you did; `assemble` refuses a section 6 written before it. You wrote them minutes ago and you will believe you remember them; you do not, and this is the one command in the step most often skipped. The output must land where you can see it: sending it to `/dev/null`, or relying on what you remember writing, is not reading. Section 6 is not a sixth part; it is what the other five add up to, and what you remember of a section is not the section. After each group, count the words (`wc -w sections/*.md`) and compare the prose against the guide's size for that section. `check.py list` prints each section's prose words against its size. Section 4 runs long in nearly every run: the calendar changes belong in the changes table, not in prose. If a section's prose is more than a quarter over, trim it now, yourself, before anyone else reads it: a long draft makes a slow critic and forces a second trim pass, and both cost the person minutes. Then run `python3 scripts/check.py list` (copy `scripts/` into the working folder first). It writes `checklist.md`: one numbered line for every sentence with a number, date, weekday, time or money in it.
2. *Not Claude? Your helper model and what to do without helpers are in `references/chatgpt.md`. The two readers and the fix pass still happen.*
   **Spawn two fresh readers in one message, so they run at the same time,** both with the model named `sonnet`, neither with any memory of the draft: a critic, who reads the writing, and a checker, who reads the facts. They read different things and neither waits on the other. They think only as hard as this session does, so each brief is a list to work through and a shape to return, never an open request to review. The critic's brief, in your words, carries all of this:
   > You are a critic. Do not rewrite; return findings only. Read `references/writing-standard.md`, then `references/section-guide.md`, then the six section files in order. For each section answer these six, in this order, one line each unless the answer is a list. One: the prose word count against the guide's size for that section, and if it is over, the cuts that bring it back, largest first, never a cut that removes a fact the reader needs to act. Two: every finding in this section that is also argued in another section, and which section should keep it; a finding with two homes is the usual cause of a long report. Three: does the first sentence give the picture, yes or no, and if no, the sentence that would. Four: up to five sentences that fail the writing standard, worst first, each with why and a plain replacement. Five: every label, metaphor or name the writer coined that the person never used. Six: anything the person would have to decode, or a question handed to them that the records could answer. Then PASS or FAIL for the section with one line of reasoning. After section 6, one line on whether the six hold one arc, and the total prose count against 2,500 to 2,900.
   The checker's brief, in your words, carries all of this:
   > You are a fact checker. You do not judge style and you do not rewrite. Read the person's answers, then `facts.md`, then `checklist.md`. Work through the checklist from the first line to the last and skip none. For each line, look the claim up: a number, date or weekday in `facts.md`; a name, an event, a time or a task by searching the index files and the answers (search, do not read them whole). Return one line per checklist number, in order, in exactly this shape: the number, then OK and where you found it, or WRONG and the correct value and where it is, or NO SOURCE. Count your lines before you return; the count must equal the checklist's. After the list, two short lists. First: every statement of how something went, how someone felt or that something happened, and every small detail nobody gave (how they will contact someone, what a class or call is about, who will be there), which neither the answers nor the records support. Second: every place where an answer and a record disagree and the section printed only one of them, including a name spelled differently from the record. If a list is empty, say so.
3. **One fix pass.** Never edit a verdict in `check-results.md` yourself; if a line is wrong, fix the section or send the line back to a checker, then run `list` and the checker again. Save the checker's list as `check-results.md` and run `python3 scripts/check.py verify`. It prints PASS or FAIL and names any line with no verdict; a block range such as "40 to 90 OK" is not a verdict. On FAIL, send the named lines back to a checker, or look them up yourself, and run it again before you change a word. Then rewrite from both sets of findings, every section. A WRONG is a fact: fix it from the source the checker named. A NO SOURCE is cut, or marked plainly as your guess, never left standing. The critic is a check, not a second writer: apply a finding when it is right, and decline it when you know something the critic could not. In the working document, list every finding you declined and why. Take the critic's size cuts unless one would cost the reader a fact they need; if the prose is still over 2,900 words after the fix, say so in the working document with the per-section counts and what you refused to cut. No second critic.

## C. Assemble and deliver

Write `header.md`: title, whose week, dates built and planned, sources, and the line "Built with the Lifehack Method's Win the Week · lifehackmethod.com". Then `python3 scripts/check.py assemble --header=header.md --file=win-the-week-YYYY-MM-DD.md`, dated the first day of the week being planned, the same date the working folder carries. It refuses unless verify passes and `references/user-canon.md` holds the setup facts; it adds the go-deeper line itself. No appendix. Ids, computations and sources stay in the working document; the report is prose for a person.

Open `prompts/03-choose-refine-write.md` now, before you write the Plan Card and the choice, not after. It holds the three endings and what each one commits you to.

Then, in the chat, three things only:

1. **The Plan Card.** The Win with its done-means and checkpoint. The bonus aims, a line each. The week's shape in one line. The blocks protected, by name. The tasks that matter. What's set aside, a clean no. A closing line in their frame: honest, a little encouraging, never a pig with lipstick.
2. **What approve would write**, under that bold heading: the exact list, one line each. Every calendar block you would add, rename or move, with its day and time. Every task you would tag, by name. Closing, moving or creating tasks is not on this list; if the plan recommends any, list them after it under "If you want these too, just say so," and do none of them on approve unless they name it. Anything with other people on it marked "I'd ask you first." If the list is long, the five that matter most and a count of the rest by kind. Nothing is on this list that is not in the report.
3. **The choice, word for word:**
   > **You get to choose what happens next**
   >
   > Nothing has been changed. Your calendar and your tasks are exactly as you left them. You have three choices.
   >
   > **1. Fix it.** If something is off, just say so. I'll ask a few short questions to find what's wrong, then show you the revised plan. We keep going until it looks right to you.
   >
   > **2. Approve it.** Say **approve** and I'll make the changes listed above under "What approve would write," and nothing else. Anything with other people on it, I confirm with you first, one at a time. Then I tell you exactly what I wrote.
   >
   > **3. Keep it as advice.** Say **advice only** and I change nothing. The plan is yours to use however you like.
   >
   > Want more on any section first? Name it and I'll go deeper.
