# The writing standard

*Part one is about understanding the thing. Part two, at the end, is about how it comes out on the page. Both apply to everything the person reads: the report, the questions, the Plan Card.*

*Folded 2026-09-14 from the plain-language skill and the weekly-report voice spec v2. Every writer reads this before writing a section. Every critic reads it before grading one.*

## The job

Understand the material so completely that the simple version becomes available to you, then write that.

Plain writing is not a style you apply at the end. It is what is left when you understand something all the way down. The extra words and the ornament were only ever covering the parts you hadn't worked out. So the first job is understanding. Think as long as it takes. Thinking is cheap. A sentence that sounds smart and explains nothing costs the reader time and attention trying to decode it, and their attention is the scarce thing, not your word count.

## The standard

Write to a smart decision-maker with no time or attention to waste. Eighth-grade sentences carrying adult ideas. Never drop the concept to make the sentence easier; explain the concept in easier words. If the material is technical and the decision rests on it, teach it as you go, a clause at a time.

Short sentences are not the same as easy reading. Chopped prose is harder to follow than prose that flows. Write the way a sharp person would say it out loud: sentences that connect, one idea leading to the next.

## The tells

These are not style crimes. Each one means you stopped understanding too early, and each is a signal to go back to the material rather than to fix the sentence.

- **"This is critical" or "the most important thing"** means you can't yet say what changes. Say what it does to them.
- **A list that stands in for analysis** means you found the items but not the pattern. Tables and lists the section guide asks for are fine; a list where a paragraph should be is the tell.
- **A long sentence with several clauses** means you're holding pieces you haven't yet related.
- **A sentence that resists being said plainly** means the idea underneath is still unresolved.
- **A word like "leverage," "second-order," or "unpack"** means you reached for a category instead of a fact. One exception: "leverage" as the name of an axis on the Eisenhack matrix is a term of the method itself, and stays.
- **An epigram or a memorable line** means you compressed before you comprehended.
- **A metaphor with nothing under it** means you haven't named the mechanism. A metaphor is welcome when it distills something real and the mechanism is named beside it. A person's own established terms (their names for their projects, their routines, their commitments) are not metaphors; use them, and say what each one is the first time.

When any of these appear, the fix is upstream. Go and understand the thing.

## Digest, don't copy

Never hand back the source in a tidier order. If a paragraph could have been assembled by copying lines from their documents, it isn't finished. Say what the material means, in your words, and let their words appear as quotes where a quote carries something yours can't.

## What to do with what won't fit

Some loss is unavoidable when you simplify. Choose what you lose, and choose confidently. The failure is dropping whatever resists the shape you picked; that is how a clean sentence quietly throws away the one detail that mattered. If something important won't fit, change the shape, not the content, or say plainly that it was left out.

## Before you return a draft

Read it as the person who has to act on it, not as the person who wrote it.

- Could they explain this back to someone else after one read?
- Does any sentence tell them something is important instead of what it does to them?
- Did I do the decoding, or hand them the raw material in a tidier order?
- Is anything here something they already knew?
- Where am I unsure, and did I say so?
- Is any of it using word count or flourish to cover for understanding I don't have?

Then the real test: if they asked you to explain any sentence more deeply, could you? If not, you wrote past your understanding, and they will feel it even if they can't name it.

## Part two: how it comes out


Part one is about understanding. It is possible to understand something completely and still write it like a clever person talking. This part is about the page itself. A reader who knows nothing about the material can check every rule here against the text.

### Size

- Say as much as the thing matters, and no more. Rank what you have. The biggest thing gets the most room; the smallest gets a line, or nothing.
- The test for every sentence, row and section: if it were cut, would the reader decide or do anything differently? If not, cut it.
- Less means fewer things said. It never means leaving out the context a sentence needs to be understood.
- Put what matters most first, so a reader who stops early still has it.
- This applies to questions and replies as much as to reports.

### Say the thing

- Every sentence names what it is about. Do not make the reader work out what "it," "that," "this one," "the ask" or "the block" points to. Write the noun, even if you wrote it two sentences ago.
  > Before: *That's why it goes first.*
  > After: *That's why the invite to Priya goes first.*
- State the full fact, not a hint at it. A sentence that only makes sense to someone who already knows the story has left the story out.
  > Before: *The ask never went.*
  > After: *The message asking Priya to cover Friday's session was never sent.*
- If you are unsure whether the reader has the context, they don't. Put it in the sentence.

### Words

- The common word over the rare one. "Use," not "leverage." "Before," not "prior to."
- The concrete name over the category. "Priya," not "your co-lead." "The note to the team," not "the handoff."
- The verb over the noun made from it. "Decide," not "make a decision." "Send," not "get it out the door."
- No term the reader would need your documents to decode. If it has to appear, say what it is the first time.
  > Before: *Your weekly plan was built Monday around a dry-run rehearsal of the board deck.*
  > After: *Monday's plan had you rehearsing the board deck this morning.*

### Sentences

- One idea per sentence. Subject and verb early. Most under twenty words. Over thirty needs a reason.
  > Before: *Your weekly plan was built Monday around a dry-run rehearsal of the board deck, scheduled for this morning, 10 AM to 12 PM, and described in that document as the only rehearsal before Thursday's meeting.*
  > After: *Monday's plan had you rehearsing the board deck this morning. That rehearsal is no longer needed.*
- No sentence that announces the next one. Cut "Here's what nobody has said out loud yet" and start with what nobody has said.
- No sentence that points back. "That's why it's ahead of the draft" makes the reader scroll up. Say the reason again in six words or cut the line.
- No sentence that ends in "is" as its own clause. "Three people who don't know the project exists is." Say it forwards.

### Paragraphs

**Eighty words is long. A hundred is too long.** Count them in the sections a reader meets first. A paragraph that runs past that is almost always two things stuck together, or a list that was written as prose. Break it, or lift the items out.

**Two long paragraphs in a row is a wall.** Vary them: a long one, then a short one, then a list. The page has to look readable before a word of it is read.

- The point first, then the reason. Never the build-up first.
- End when the point is made. A paragraph does not need a last line.
- No closing line written to be quoted. One quotable line in a report can land. Twenty in a row is a voice performing, and the reader tires.
  > Before: *There's no calendar entry, no thread, no task. It's entirely inside your head, which is exactly the shape a thing has right before it doesn't happen.*
  > After: *There's no calendar entry, no thread, no task. It only exists in your head.*

### The document

- Every finding is worked out once, in the section it belongs to. Elsewhere it is referred to, not re-argued.
- Context is different from a finding, and context repeats. If a sentence only makes sense because Maya's surgery is at 7 AM, say so in that sentence, even if the surgery was named two sections ago. Brief is not the same as clear. A reader who has to remember what you said earlier, or guess at it, has been handed less than they need. When in doubt, restate the context.
- Two tables are never the same table. If seven rows match, there is one table.

### Adornment

- Scan the page for anything there to sound good rather than to say something: a flourish, a rhythm, a second image, a vivid word where a plain one was available. Cut it. "Scheduled on top of an operating table" is adornment. "Scheduled during Maya's surgery" is the fact.
- A metaphor only when the plain sentence can't carry it, and the mechanism beside it. One per report is enough. "A parking lot with a time painted on it" is a good line and it is still the second metaphor in that section.
- Level tone. Not urgent, not clever. Name what it does to them, never how important it is.
- **No coined labels.** Call things what the person calls them. "Client calls," not "a delivery day." "Two half-hour gaps," not "the seams."
- **No set-ups.** "Not by anything blowing up." "The tell is simple:" "Here's the thing." Cut the wind-up and state the fact.
- **No raised stakes.** Don't attach a threat to an item unless the threat is real and changes what they do. "Left alone, it becomes someone else's problem by Monday" is drama. "Priya can't confirm the dates until you send them" is a fact.
- **The out-loud test.** Would you say this sentence to them across a table? If not, say it the way you would.
