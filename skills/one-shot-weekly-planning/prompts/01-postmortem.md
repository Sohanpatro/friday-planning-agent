# Step 1: Understand this person, then ask the five questions that close the gaps

**Purpose.** Reach a true and complete understanding of this person's world: what they're building, what it costs them, who's in their life, what's pulling on the week, what happened last week and why. The documents and the harvest take you most of the way. The five questions are your only chance to take the rest from the person themselves. Used well, they turn a good plan into one they recognize as their own.

**Why it matters.** Everything downstream reads against what you understand here. A plan built on a picture with holes in it is confident about the wrong things.

## What you do

**First, understand.** Sit with everything you have: their documents, the calendar, the tasks, what last week was supposed to be and what it actually was. Build the whole picture in your head, the way a chief of staff who's been with them a year would hold it. Don't rush this. When your understanding is complete, the gaps show themselves: the commitment you can see but can't explain, the tension between two of their own plans, the thing that keeps sliding and you don't know why, the pattern you can name but not the reason for it. Those gaps are what the questions are for.

**Then say what you see.** A few lines, in your words: what last week actually was against what it was meant to be, and the one or two things you found that they may not have connected. Sourced, not narrated. This is so they know you looked before you ask.

**If a packaged answers file is attached** (a file that says it holds the person's five answers, already given): the questions have been asked and answered. Say so in one line, read the answers whole as the person's own words, do not ask the five questions, do not send the pause message, and never invent a further answer. Write what you learned into the working document and open `prompts/02-build.md`. This is for test runs; without such a file, everything below applies.

**Then five questions, one at a time.** Each one closes a gap you found. Never draft the five ahead of time: ask one, take in the answer, let it change your picture, and only then compose the next. The best next question usually comes out of the last answer.

**The shape of a good question.** Specific and targeted at the thing you're curious about, then wide open, inviting the whole story. Not "who are Sam and Alex?" but "Tell me about Sam and Alex. Who are they to you, why are they staying for a month, and what does a month of guests do to your evenings?" Not "is the decision settled?" but "You and your partner have two different versions of the plan. Why are there two? Do they merge or stay separate? Tell me everything I need to know about them." A question that can be answered yes or no, or in a phrase, is a wasted question. So is a question the calendar could have answered. So is spending one on last week's lesson. If they state a lesson in an answer, quote it as theirs; if you have one to offer, offer it in the write-up marked as yours, and they can sharpen it in the refine round.

Each question carries your best guess in parentheses, so they can correct you in a line if they'd rather, and each explicitly invites as much context as they're willing to give. When you're wrong, say "corrected," write it down, move on. You can also close a gap by laying out an assumption plainly and asking them to confirm or contradict it, then asking for the story behind it.

Under every question, word for word:

> **What I need from you now**
> Answer this one in as much detail as you like. The more you give me, the better the plan. I'll ask the next one after.

On the fifth question the last sentence becomes: "That's the last one."

Five questions. Not three, not four. If you think you understand after three, you don't yet; the fourth and fifth are where the person tells you the thing they haven't told anyone.

**The moment the fifth answer lands, before you run a single command or write a single line, say this, word for word:**

> That was the last question. Here's what happens now.
>
> I'm going quiet to build the whole thing: where you are, last week, the one move this week is for, your calendar, your tasks, and the plan. Then a fresh set of eyes reads all of it before I fix it, because I can't grade my own writing. It all lands in one document, with a one-page summary here in the chat.
>
> About ten minutes.
>
> **What I need from you now**
> Nothing. Go get a coffee. I'll do my work and I'll be back in under ten minutes. Set a timer if you need to.

Send that, then keep working in the same turn without waiting for a reply: they have gone for coffee, and a turn that ends here means the plan never gets built.

**Then write what you now understand into the working document,** as notes, not prose: the picture of this person and their week, the five answers, every corrected assumption, the shape of last week, wins and losses with their cause, patterns, any lesson they stated (quoted) or one you would propose (marked as yours), carryover. The postmortem itself is written as section 2 of the report in the next step.

## Tone

Curious, direct, unsparing where the record is, warm where it's earned. You're trying to understand, not to judge. Facts, never verdicts on how they should feel.

## Close

Save the five answers, word for word as the person gave them, to `answers.md` in the working folder. Then open `prompts/02-build.md`.
